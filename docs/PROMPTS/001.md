## 2. Texture Decision (04:24 UTC)

> In this case I want to keep thing simple. Texture is just part of the world.

**What happened:** Blaine resolved the first design tension immediately — world-building "texture" (lore, recurring jokes, artifacts) lives on characters and locations as freeform descriptions, not as a separate entity. Set the tone for the whole session: simplicity over structure.

<details>
<summary><strong>Response:</strong> That makes sense — and it aligns with how the GENESYS describes texture. It's not a separate *thing*, it's the connective tissue of the world.</summary>

That makes sense — and it aligns with how the GENESYS describes texture. It's not a separate *thing*, it's the connective tissue of the world.

So texture lives on characters and locations as rich descriptions rather than as its own entity. The AI pulls from those descriptions at generation time.

But here's the wrinkle — the GENESYS also describes **lore generation after stories**. When a story is generated, the system produces lore artifacts — "a character said something memorable, a new detail about a place was invented, an object appeared." The user reviews them and keeps the ones they like.

That *does* need to be its own entity, because it has a lifecycle: generated → reviewed → kept or discarded. And kept lore feeds into future stories.

So I'd propose a distinction:

- **World-building texture** — lives on characters and locations as rich description fields. You write this stuff directly.
- **Lore artifacts** — generated *from* stories, stored separately, with a status (pending/kept/discarded). These are the emergent bits that accumulate over time.

A kept lore artifact might say "The blacksmith always taps his hammer three times before starting work" — it's connected to the blacksmith character, born from a specific story, and available to future stories.

Does that split feel right to you? Texture you author is part of the entity. Texture the system discovers gets its own table with provenance.

Multiple worlds means you could have a medieval fantasy world *and* a sci-fi world and they stay separate. But it adds a layer to every query and every URL (`/worlds/:id/characters` vs just `/characters`).

<details>
<summary><strong>Response:</strong> That's a really good question. Let me push back a little before we collapse it.</summary>

That's a really good question. Let me push back a little before we collapse it.

Locations and characters serve different roles at generation time. A character has agency — they *do* things, they have voice, personality, relationships. A location is a stage — it sets mood, provides sensory anchors, constrains what's plausible.

If you fold locations into lore, you lose the ability to say "set this story at the forge" as a first-class action. It becomes "here's a lore artifact that describes a place called the forge" — which the AI can work with, but it's fuzzier.

But I hear what you're saying. You're not imagining locations as detailed dungeon maps. You're imagining them as *vibes* — the town, the woods nearby, the mountains on the horizon. Atmospheric anchors more than distinct settings.

Here's a middle path: **keep Location as its own entity, but keep it lightweight.** A name, a description, and that's about it. No complex fields, no coordinates, no sub-locations. It exists so you can:

1. Select it when generating a story ("set this in the woods")
2. Attach lore artifacts to it ("the woods are where the lanterns appear at night")
3. Give the AI a distinct block of context that's clearly "place" vs "person"

That's barely more work than a lore entry, but it preserves the semantic distinction. If it turns out you never use it as a separate pick, no harm done.

Keep it as a lightweight entity, or fold it into lore?

</details>

Great questions. Let me break it down.

**Is it complicated?** Not really — it's two small join tables. `StoryCharacter` (story_id, character_id) and `StoryLocation` (story_id, location_id). You've written a hundred of these. The schema is trivial.

**Does it show up to the user?** Two places:

1. **At generation time** — the user picks characters and optionally a location. Those selections *are* the join records. So the user is already managing this without knowing it — they're just choosing who's in the story.
2. **After generation** — on a character's page you could show "Stories featuring this character." On a story page, you see who was in it. That's a nice browse experience and it's basically free once the links exist.

**Who manages it?** The user, implicitly. When they say "tell me a story about the blacksmith and the librarian at the tavern," we create the story and the links. No extra step, no admin screen. It's infrastructure that powers a good UX but doesn't add any burden.

The AI's role is consuming these links — when generating a new story, it can look at previous stories involving the same characters and maintain continuity. "Last time these two met, the librarian was angry about the overdue book. Let's reference that."

So: low implementation cost, no user-facing complexity, high value for story coherence.
